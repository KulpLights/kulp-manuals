    Kulp Lights Controller Manual¶

    This manual covers the hardware of the Kulp Lights controllers.

    • K8-Pro · K8-Max

      8 local pixel ports + 4 differential ports (16 strings).

      K8-Pro · K8-Max

    • K8-PB · K4-PB · K40D-PB

      Compact: 8 or 4 local pixel ports, or 40 strings on 10 differential ports (K40D-PB).

      K8-PB · K4-PB · K40D-PB

    • K16 · K16-Pro · K16-Max

      16 local pixel ports + 4 or 8 differential ports.

      K16 · K16-Pro · K16-Max

    • K32 · K32-Pro · K32-Max

      32 local pixel ports + 4 or 8 differential ports.

      K32 · K32-Pro · K32-Max

    • PocketScroller v2 · v3 · -16

      6, 8 or 16 HUB75 outputs for LED panel matrices.

      PocketScroller v2 · v3 · PocketScroller-16

    BeagleBone Black controllers¶

    • K8-B · K8-B-Scroller

      8 fused pixel ports + differential ports; the K8-B-Scroller adds 3 HUB75 panel outputs.

      K8-B · K8-B-Scroller

    • K64D-B · K128D-B

      64 or 128 strings on 16 or 32 differential ports, with audio and two DMX/LOR outputs.

      K64D-B · K128D-B

    • OctoPlus

      8 HUB75 outputs for LED panel matrices.

      OctoPlus

    Raspberry Pi controllers¶

    • K8-Pi

      8 eFused pixel ports + 3 differential ports and an expansion port: 36 strings.

      K8-Pi

    • K2-Pi-Servo · K2-Pi0

      2 eFused pixel ports, plus 16 servo outputs (K2-Pi-Servo) or on a Pi Zero (K2-Pi0).

      K2-Pi-Servo · K2-Pi0

    Displays & add-ons¶

    • BBB-OLED · Pi-OLED

      A status display, buttons and a clock for a BeagleBone or Pi running FPP. The PiOLED 3.0 adds high-quality audio.

      BBB-OLED · Pi-OLED

    Every one of these boards is a cape (or hat) for a PocketBeagle 2, a BeagleBone Black or a Raspberry Pi and runs FPP (Falcon Player). FPP is the software that plays your show, and it has its own manual at manual.falconplayer.com. This manual doesn't repeat that one. It covers what the FPP manual can't:

    • Power: wiring power supplies, banks, fuses and eFuses.
    • Pixel outputs and differential ports: wiring pixels and receivers.
    • Controls & display: the buttons, the OLED menus and the status LEDs.
    • Board pages: one per controller, with specifications, connector maps and dimensions.
    • Troubleshooting for hardware problems.

    Read the Safety page before you connect a power supply.

    Getting Started

    Safety¶

    These controllers run on low-voltage DC (5–24 V) but can switch a lot of current. A K32 with every port loaded can deliver well over 100 A across its banks. At that level, the hazards are fire from loose or undersized wiring, and the mains (AC) side of your power supplies.

    Mains voltage

    • Never connect household AC (120/230 V) to any terminal on the controller. The controller only accepts DC from a power supply.
    • Have the AC side of your power supplies wired by someone qualified. Use grounded (3-prong) cords and close the terminal covers on the supply.
    • If a supply has a 115V/230V input switch, set it to match your mains before you plug it in. The wrong setting can destroy the supply.
    • Unplug the AC before you touch the supply's output terminals or the controller's power terminals.

    Fire prevention

    • Never fit a larger fuse than the one supplied, and never bridge a fuse holder. The fuse protects the pixel wiring; a bigger fuse lets that wiring overheat.
    • Use wire rated for the current it carries (see Power → Wire size). Tighten every screw terminal firmly. A loose terminal heats up under load.
    • Tin or ferrule stranded wire ends. Stray strands can short to the next terminal.
    • Mount the controller in an enclosure, away from flammable material. Leave room for airflow, especially around the power terminals and the eFuse/fuse area.

    Polarity and voltage

    • Check V+ and G/V− on every terminal before you power up. On the K8, K16 and K32 boards the power inputs have reverse-polarity protection, so reversed power won't damage the controller; that bank simply stays off. The K8-PB and K4-PB have no such protection, and a reversed supply can damage them. Pixels have no such protection either. Wiring a pixel string backwards, or running 5 V pixels from a 12 V bank, will destroy the pixels.
    • Every pixel port on a bank gets the voltage of that bank's power supply. Check that the supply voltage matches the pixels on every port in the bank.
    • The K40D-PB and the PocketScrollers accept 5 V only. Do not connect 12 V or 24 V to them.

    Outdoors

    The controller is not waterproof. Outdoors, mount it in a weather-resistant enclosure with drip loops on every cable, and keep the power supplies in an enclosure too. Condensation and insects cause more failures than rain does.

    Making connections

    Turn the power off before you plug or unplug pixel connectors, receivers or power terminals. Network, USB, audio and DMX cables can be connected with the power on.

    Storage

    If you store the controller for a long time, take out the clock (RTC) coin cell. That keeps it from draining and leaking.

    Quick start¶

    Work through this list in order. Each step finishes with a check, so if something goes wrong you know which step it was.

    What you need¶

    • [ ] The controller, with a PocketBeagle 2, BeagleBone Black or Raspberry Pi fitted (see PocketBeagle 2, BeagleBone Black or Raspberry Pi)
    • [ ] A microSD card with FPP, unless you have a PB2 Industrial with FPP on eMMC
    • [ ] A 5–24 V DC LED power supply matched to your pixels (5 V only for the PocketScrollers, K40D-PB, K64D-B, K128D-B and OctoPlus; 18 V at most for the K8-PB, K4-PB, K8-B, K8-B-Scroller and K8-Pi)
    • [ ] An Ethernet cable to your router or show switch. The K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB and PocketScroller v2 have no Ethernet jack, so they also need a USB Ethernet adapter (or a USB Wi-Fi adapter).
    • [ ] Pixels with a pigtail wired to a 3-pin plug (or LED panels for the PocketScrollers and OctoPlus)
    • [ ] A small flat screwdriver

    1. Bench test with one string¶

    On the K40D-PB, K64D-B and K128D-B, which have no pixel ports, wire the string to a differential receiver on the 1-4 jack instead, and power the board from a 5 V supply.

    1. [ ] Read Safety.
    2. [ ] Insert the SD card into the PocketBeagle 2, BeagleBone or Pi, unless you are using eMMC.
    3. [ ] Wire one pixel string to P1: ground in G, data in D, +V in V. Check the pixel voltage matches your supply.
    4. [ ] Check the supply before connecting it: set its 115V/230V input switch (if it has one) to match your mains, then plug it in and measure the DC output with a multimeter. Unplug it again before wiring. See Check the supply.
    5. [ ] Wire the supply to the terminal that powers the controller. That's the terminal for ports 1–4 on the K16 and K32, and the right-hand terminal on the K8 boards. On the K8 boards, P1 is fed from the left terminal, so for this test wire both K8 terminals, or use P5 instead. The K8-PB, K4-PB, K8-B, K8-B-Scroller, K8-Pi and K2-Pi0 have a single terminal for everything; on the K2-Pi-Servo, use the top (pixel) terminal. See Power.
    6. [ ] Plug in the Ethernet cable (on the K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB or PocketScroller v2, through the USB Ethernet adapter).
    7. [ ] Plug the supply into the mains.

    Check: the power LEDs light (on the K8-B and K8-B-Scroller, the green LED above each fuse), and within a minute or two the OLED shows the FPP logo and then the status page with an IP address. The first boot of a new SD card can take a few minutes longer.

    2. Connect to FPP¶

    1. [ ] On a computer on the same network, open a browser to the IP address on the OLED, or to http://fpp.local.
    2. [ ] Work through FPP's first-time setup: hostname, time zone and so on. See Initial configuration.

    Check: the FPP status page shows your board under the cape name, and the header shows the input voltage(s).

    3. Light the string¶

    1. [ ] Press Test on the controller.

    Check: the string on P1 cycles red, green and blue. Press Test until the OLED shows Off.

    If nothing lights, see Troubleshooting → No pixels light.

    4. Configure your show¶

    1. [ ] In xLights, add the controller as an FPP controller with your board as the model, map your props to its ports, and use Upload Outputs to send the port configuration. See Configuring outputs.
    2. [ ] Upload sequences and audio with FPP Connect, then build a playlist and a schedule in FPP.

    5. Install¶

    1. [ ] Main Menu → Shutdown (or shut down from FPP), then remove power.
    2. [ ] Mount the controller in its enclosure. Wire the remaining banks and ports, then the differential receivers.
    3. [ ] Power up and run the Test patterns again to check every port.

    Back up

    Once it's working, save a backup from FPP (Status/Control → FPP Backup). See Backup.

    Hardware

    PocketBeagle 2¶

    Every board in this manual is a cape for the BeagleBoard PocketBeagle 2 (PB2). The PB2 is a small computer that runs FPP, and the cape adds the power, outputs and connectors.

    PocketBeagle 2 only

    These capes do not work with the original PocketBeagle (PocketBeagle 1). The two look alike, but the PocketBeagle 2 has a different processor and pinout. The exceptions are the K4-PB, K40D-PB and PocketScroller v2, which work with either.

    Regular or Industrial¶

    Kulp Lights sells the boards with or without a PB2 already fitted:

    PocketBeagle 2 PocketBeagle 2 Industrial
    Board colour black red
    Memory 512 MB 1 GB
    Storage microSD card 64 GB eMMC with FPP pre-installed, microSD optional
    Temperature range commercial −40 to 85 °C
    Notes the usual choice eMMC is about twice as fast as an SD card. Recommended for outdoor or heavily loaded shows.

    Fitting the PocketBeagle 2¶

    The PocketBeagle 2 fits the same way round on every board in this manual: its USB-C connector faces the right-hand edge of the board, the edge opposite the buttons and the Exp header. Its P1 header goes into the cape socket marked P1 (the one nearer the Ethernet and USB connectors; on the K8-PB, the upper one), and P2 into P2.

    If your board came without a PB2 fitted:

    1. Disconnect all power.
    2. On the K8 boards the OLED sits over the PocketBeagle. Unplug it from its 4-pin socket first, removing its standoff screws if fitted.
    3. Hold the PB2 with its USB-C connector toward the right-hand edge, and line its pins up with both sockets.
    4. Press it down evenly, rocking gently end to end, until it is fully seated. Don't press on the processor.
    5. Refit the OLED.

    Orientation

    A PB2 plugged in backwards, or shifted by a row, can be damaged at power-up. Before you power on, check that the USB-C faces the right-hand edge and that no pins are left over at either end.

    Storage: SD card or eMMC¶

    • Regular PB2: FPP runs from a microSD card. Use a good-quality card of 16–32 GB (A1/A2, U3). Cheap or counterfeit cards are the most common cause of strange problems. You can buy a card with FPP already installed, or write the PocketBeagle 2 image yourself. See Getting the FPP software.
    • PB2 Industrial: FPP is pre-installed on the internal eMMC, so no SD card is needed.
    • A microSD extender lets you reach the card slot when the board is in an enclosure.

    SD card in a PB2 Industrial¶

    A PB2 Industrial always starts from its eMMC, but the bootloader there checks for a valid SD card first and boots from the card if it finds one. So:

    • Don't leave an old FPP card in the slot of an Industrial board. It will boot the card, not the eMMC.
    • To run a different FPP version or a clean install for a while, just insert a card with FPP on it. Take the card out to go back to the eMMC.

    Re-flashing the eMMC¶

    To put a fresh copy of FPP on the eMMC, for example to start clean or to recover a broken install:

    1. Write the PocketBeagle 2 FPP image to an SD card. See Getting the FPP software.
    2. Insert the card and power up. The board boots from the card.
    3. In FPP, go to Status/Control → FPP Settings → Storage. Under Flash FPP to Another Device, find the eMMC (/dev/mmcblk0) and choose:
      • Create for a clean install. Your media, sequences and settings are left behind.
      • Copy to bring your media, sequences and settings along.
    4. Wait for it to finish. This can take a while. Then power off, remove the SD card, and power up again from the eMMC.

    If the board won't boot at all

    If the eMMC's bootloader is damaged, the board can't find the SD card on its own. Hold the PocketBeagle 2's boot button while you apply power to force it to boot from the SD card, then re-flash as above. This is very rarely needed.

    You don't need to re-flash to update FPP. FPP and OS updates work in place on the eMMC; see Updating. Re-flashing is only for a clean start or recovery.

    The PocketBeagle 2's buttons¶

    The PocketBeagle 2 has two small buttons of its own, side by side, separate from the buttons on the cape. They aren't labelled on the board:

    Button Where What it does
    Power the one nearest the board edge and the PocketBeagle's LEDs Holding it down turns the PocketBeagle 2 off. Don't hold it by accident; to turn off cleanly, use FPP's Shutdown instead.
    Boot the one beside it, further from the edge Only read at power-on. Hold it while you apply power to change where the board boots from (below).

    What the boot button does depends on the model:

    Normal power-on Boot button held at power-on
    PocketBeagle 2 Industrial boots from the eMMC. The bootloader there then switches to an SD card if a valid one is inserted. boots straight from the SD card, skipping the eMMC. Use this to recover a damaged eMMC.
    PocketBeagle 2 (regular) boots from the SD card also boots from the SD card. The button only changes the fallback the chip tries if the card can't be read, so it has no practical use.

    Using the boot button as an input¶

    The boot button only affects booting at power-on, so once FPP is running you can use it as a spare push button. Starting with FPP 10.4, it appears in FPP's GPIO Inputs as BOOT. Set it up like any other input to start a playlist, run a command or trigger an effect. See GPIO Inputs in the FPP manual.

    Pressing it while the controller is running is safe: it doesn't reboot or change anything until the next power-on.

    Powering¶

    The PB2 is powered from the cape, through its power terminal (see Power).

    USB-C

    Don't use the PB2's USB-C port to power the controller. USB-C powers only the PocketBeagle 2 itself, not the cape's Ethernet, USB ports or outputs. You can still connect it to a computer for setup (USB tethering), except on the K40D-PB, where that can damage the board.

    BeagleBone Black¶

    The K8-B, K8-B-Scroller, K64D-B, K128D-B and OctoPlus, and the retired K16A-B and K32A-B, are capes for the BeagleBone Black (BBB). The BBB is a small computer that runs FPP, and the cape adds the power, outputs and connectors. FPP drives the outputs from the BBB's two PRUs, small real-time processors beside the main CPU.

    Which BeagleBones work¶

    Board Works? Notes
    BeagleBone Black ✔ the usual choice
    BeagleBone Black Industrial ✔ the same as the BeagleBone Black, with a wider temperature rating
    BeagleBone Black Wireless ✔ Wi-Fi instead of Ethernet
    BeagleBone Green ✔ no barrel jack (it's powered from the cape anyway)
    SanCloud BeagleBone Enhanced ✔ on the OctoPlus, output 5 doesn't work properly
    BeagleBone Green Wireless ✘ not compatible
    PocketBeagle, PocketBeagle 2, BeagleBone AI ✘ different boards. See PocketBeagle 2.

    Kulp Lights sells these boards with or without a BeagleBone Black (or Green) already fitted, and with or without an SD card with FPP on it.

    Fitting the BeagleBone¶

    The cape connects to the BeagleBone's two 46-pin headers, P8 and P9. How they fit together depends on the board:

    • K8-B, K8-B-Scroller and OctoPlus: the cape plugs on top of the BeagleBone. The BeagleBone's Ethernet jack sits in the notch cut out of the cape's edge, so the cape only fits one way round.
    • K64D-B and K128D-B: these capes are much bigger, so the BeagleBone plugs upside down onto the cape, with its Ethernet jack over the notch in the cape's right-hand edge. Its microSD slot then faces up, and its USB-A port is still usable.

    If your board came without a BeagleBone fitted:

    1. Disconnect all power.
    2. Line the BeagleBone's Ethernet jack up with the notch in the cape, and both rows of header pins with both sockets.
    3. Press evenly along both headers, rocking gently end to end, until the two boards are fully together.

    Check before you power on

    A cape that is shifted by a row, or fitted the wrong way round, can damage both boards. Check that every pin is in its socket and that none are left over at either end.

    Storage: run FPP from an SD card¶

    Run FPP from a microSD card in the BeagleBone. Use a good-quality card of 16–32 GB (A1/A2, U3). Cheap or counterfeit cards are the most common cause of strange problems. You can buy a card with FPP already installed, or write the BeagleBone Black image yourself. See Getting the FPP software. Use FPP 9.5 or later; the current release is best.

    The BeagleBone also has built-in eMMC storage, but on most boards it is only 4 GB, too small to hold a show's media comfortably, so we recommend the SD card. (BeagleBone Blacks made since late 2025 have 16 GB of eMMC. FPP's Status/Control → FPP Settings → Storage page can copy FPP to it.)

    Booting from the SD card¶

    BeagleBones made since about 2019 boot from an SD card on their own: insert the card and power up.

    Older boards boot whatever is on their eMMC instead. To boot one of those from the SD card, hold the Boot button while you apply power, and keep holding it until the BeagleBone's LEDs start flashing. The K64D-B and K128D-B have a Boot button on the cape for this. On the other boards, use the BeagleBone's own Boot button, next to its USB-A port.

    Powering¶

    The BeagleBone is powered from the cape: the cape's power terminal feeds the BeagleBone through its header pins (see Power).

    Don't power the BeagleBone a second way

    • Don't plug a supply into the BeagleBone's barrel jack while the cape is powering it. The two supplies end up tied together. (The K8-B and K8-B-Scroller have a jumper that lets you power the BeagleBone from its barrel jack instead; see their pages.)
    • Don't connect the BeagleBone's USB port to a computer (USB tethering) while the cape is powered. Set the controller up over the network instead.

    Power button¶

    Each cape has a Power button wired to the BeagleBone's own power button, which is hard to reach once a cape is fitted:

    • When FPP is running, a short press shuts the BeagleBone down cleanly, the same as FPP's Shutdown.
    • After a shutdown, the BeagleBone stays off while the cape is still powered. Press Power to start it again, or switch the supply off and on.
    • Holding it down for several seconds forces the BeagleBone off. Use that only if it has hung.

    Network and USB¶

    • Ethernet: the BeagleBone's own 10/100 Ethernet jack. The BeagleBone Black Wireless has Wi-Fi instead.
    • USB: the BeagleBone has one USB-A port, for a USB sound card, Wi-Fi adapter or USB storage.
    • Status LEDs: the BeagleBone's four blue LEDs show it booting and running.

    Raspberry Pi¶

    The K8-Pi, K2-Pi-Servo and K2-Pi0 are hats for the Raspberry Pi. The Pi runs FPP, and the hat adds the power, outputs and connectors.

    Which Raspberry Pis work¶

    Board Raspberry Pi
    K8-Pi, K2-Pi-Servo Pi 3 B+, Pi 4 or Pi 5. The hat's mounting holes match these Pis.
    K2-Pi0 Pi Zero 2 W, the board it is designed for. It also works on an original Pi Zero W and on the bigger Pis, but see the warning on its board page.

    Kulp Lights sells the boards with or without a Pi, and with or without an SD card with FPP on it.

    Fitting the hat¶

    The hat has a 40-pin socket on its underside that plugs onto the Pi's GPIO header.

    1. Disconnect all power.
    2. Screw M2.5 standoffs, nylon or metal, into the Pi's four mounting holes.
    3. Line the socket up with all 40 of the Pi's header pins, and press the hat down evenly until it is fully seated on the header and resting on the standoffs.
    4. Fix the hat to the standoffs with M2.5 screws.

    Check before you power on

    A hat that is shifted by a row or a column of pins can damage the Pi. Check that every pin is in the socket and none are left over. The standoffs keep the hat from bending down onto the Pi's USB and Ethernet ports; don't leave them out.

    The hat sits close over the Pi, so a tall heatsink or fan on the Pi may not fit underneath it. Use a low-profile cooler.

    Storage¶

    FPP runs from a microSD card in the Pi. Use a good-quality card of 16–32 GB (A1/A2, U3), and the Raspberry Pi FPP image. See Getting the FPP software. Use FPP 10 or later; the current release is best.

    Powering¶

    The hat powers the Pi: its own regulator turns the supply on the power terminal into 5 V and feeds it to the Pi through the GPIO header.

    Don't power the Pi a second way

    • Don't plug a supply into the Pi's own USB power socket while the hat is powered. The two supplies end up tied together. (The K8-Pi has a jumper that lets you power the Pi from its own supply instead; see its page.)
    • Don't connect the Pi's USB to a computer (USB tethering) while the hat is powered. Set the controller up over the network instead.

    Using a 5 V supply

    The hats' 5 V regulator can't quite hold 5 V when its input is only 5 V, so the Pi may report under-voltage. If you power one from a 5 V supply, turn the supply's output up to about 5.3 V (most LED supplies have an adjustment screw).

    There is no power button on the hats. To turn off, use FPP's Shutdown, then switch off the supply.

    Pixel outputs on a Pi¶

    FPP drives the pixel ports through the Pi's display output, with its DPIPixels output:

    • Each string can have up to 1600 pixels. The frame rate is set by the longest string: about 40 fps at 800 pixels and 20 fps at 1600, and faster for shorter strings.
    • That variable frame rate arrived in FPP 10. FPP 9.5 runs only at 20 or 40 fps.
    • Smart receivers on the K8-Pi's differential ports work in v1 or v2 mode only.
    • The Pi's own audio output still works alongside the pixel outputs.

    Network¶

    The Pi 3 B+, Pi 4 and Pi 5 have built-in Ethernet and Wi-Fi. The Pi Zero 2 W has Wi-Fi only (2.4 GHz); set it up with FPP's Wi-Fi hotspot.

    Power¶

    Banks¶

    The local pixel ports are grouped in banks of four. Each bank gets its power from one power input terminal. Every port in a bank receives that terminal's voltage, so a bank must be wired to a supply that matches the pixels on all four of its ports.

    • Each bank can have its own supply and its own voltage. Run 12 V pixels on one bank and 5 V pixels on another, or 24 V on a third: 5 V, 12 V and 24 V pixels can share one controller as long as they are on different banks, each fed from a supply that matches its pixels.
    • All grounds (G/V−) are connected together on the board.
    • The differential ports carry data only. Strings on differential receivers are powered at the receiver.

    The terminals are marked on the board V+ 5-24V and G/V− (V+ 5-18V and G on the K8-PB, V+ and GND on the K4-PB, K8-B and K8-B-Scroller, V+ and G on the Pi boards). Each board page shows which terminal feeds which ports.

    Board Power terminals Bank size Terminal that also powers the controller

    K8-PB 1 (5–18 V) all 8 ports the only terminal
    K4-PB 1 (5 or 12 V, 18 V max) all 4 ports the only terminal
    K40D-PB 1 (5 V only) — (differential only) the only terminal
    K16, K16-Pro, K16-Max 4 4 ports the upper-left terminal (ports 1–4)
    K32, K32-Pro, K32-Max 8 4 ports the top-left terminal (ports 1–4)
    PocketScroller v2, v3 and -16 1 (5 V only) — the only terminal
    K8-B, K8-B-Scroller 1 (5–18 V) all 8 ports the only terminal
    K64D-B, K128D-B, OctoPlus 1 (5 V only) — the only terminal
    K8-Pi 1 (5–18 V) all 8 ports the only terminal
    K16A-B 2 (v1, v2) or 4 (v3) 8 or 4 ports the right-hand terminal
    K32A-B 4 8 ports the bottom-right terminal
    K2-Pi0 1 (5–24 V) both ports the only terminal
    K2-Pi-Servo 2: pixels (5–24 V) and servos both pixel ports the top (pixel) terminal

    One terminal also runs the controller

    The PocketBeagle 2 or BeagleBone, network, USB, OLED and all of the controller's logic run from one specific power terminal (the last column above). If that terminal has no power, the controller won't boot, even with every other bank powered. The other terminals power only their own pixel ports.

    On the PocketBeagle 2 pixel controllers, a blue LED next to that terminal lights when it has power, and a second blue LED shows that the controller's internal 5 V supply is running.

    Voltage¶

    • K8, K16 and K32 boards: each terminal accepts 5 V to 24 V DC (as printed next to the terminal). Use a regulated 5 V, 12 V or 24 V LED power supply. Do not go above 24 V; there is no extra headroom for 28 V or 36 V supplies.
    • K8-PB and K4-PB: one terminal, 5 V to 18 V DC (5 or 12 V supplies), for the controller and all the ports. No 24 V. See the K8-PB and K4-PB pages.
    • K40D-PB: 5 V DC only, for the controller alone, from a supply rated 4–5 A. Its strings are powered at the receivers.
    • PocketScroller v2, v3 and -16: 5 V DC only. See their board pages.
    • K8-B and K8-B-Scroller: one terminal, 5 V to 18 V DC, for the controller and all the ports. No 24 V.
    • K8-Pi: one terminal, 5 V to 18 V DC, for the Pi and all the ports. No 24 V.
    • K2-Pi-Servo and K2-Pi0: 5 V to 24 V DC for the Pi and the pixel ports. The K2-Pi-Servo's servos have their own terminal, typically 5–6 V.
    • Raspberry Pi boards on 5 V: turn the supply up to about 5.3 V; see Raspberry Pi → Powering.
    • K64D-B, K128D-B and OctoPlus: 5 V DC only, for the controller alone (3 A or more for the K64D-B, 4 A or more for the K128D-B). Their strings are powered at the receivers, and the OctoPlus's panels from their own supply.
    • A 5 V supply on the logic terminal is fine. The controller's internal 5 V regulator passes it straight through (about 4.96 V out from 5.0 V in), so 5 V-only setups need no separate supply.

    Reverse polarity¶

    On the K8, K16 and K32 boards each power input has an ideal diode in series. If a supply is wired backwards, that bank simply stays off; nothing on the controller is damaged. Fix the wiring and the bank will come up.

    The K8-PB and K4-PB are not protected this way: a reversed supply can damage them. On the K8-B and K8-B-Scroller the BeagleBone and the logic are protected, but a reversed supply goes straight through the fuses to the pixel ports. The K64D-B isn't protected either. On the Raspberry Pi boards the Pi and the logic are protected, but the pixel ports aren't. Check the polarity before you power up. Pixels have no such protection; see Safety.

    Voltage readings in FPP¶

    FPP shows each input voltage in its status bar and on the OLED status pages (VIN1, VIN2, …). The terminals aren't numbered on the board, so each board page has a map of which VIN reading belongs to which terminal. They follow the port numbers: VIN1 is the terminal for ports 1–4 (1–8 on boards with eight ports per terminal), VIN2 the next bank, and so on.

    Choosing power supplies¶

    • Use regulated, constant-voltage LED power supplies (often sold as 350 W 5 V/12 V/24 V "Meanwell-style" units). Constant-current LED drivers are not suitable.
    • Size the supply for the pixels, not the controller. The controller itself needs only a few watts.
    • Pixel current depends on brightness and colour. A full-white string draws its full rated current; most shows average far less. Common practice is to size each supply for about 60–80 % of the pixels' full-white load, and to cap brightness in FPP or xLights if needed.
    • One supply can feed several banks, as long as each wire run is sized for its current.

    Check the supply before you connect it¶

    Do this for every supply, new or used, before you wire it to the controller:

    1. Set the AC input switch. Many supplies have a small slide switch, often red, marked 115V / 230V (or 110V / 220V). Set it to match your mains: 115 V in North America, 230 V in most of Europe, Asia and Australia. A supply set to 115 V and plugged into 230 V mains is destroyed at once, and can catch fire. One set to 230 V on 115 V mains won't start or gives a low output. Supplies without the switch accept either.
    2. Measure the output. With nothing connected to the DC side, plug the supply in and measure across its V+ and V− (COM) terminals with a multimeter. Check it reads the voltage your pixels need (5, 12 or 24 V) and that + and − are where you expect. If it is slightly off, most supplies have a V.ADJ trim pot next to the terminals.
    3. Unplug the supply and wait for its LED to go out before you wire it to the controller.

    Wire size¶

    Use copper wire sized for the highest current that wire can carry. That means the bank's total for supply-to-terminal wires, and the port fuse or eFuse rating for pixel leads.

    Current (continuous) Minimum wire
    up to 5 A (one port) 18 AWG (0.75 mm²)
    up to 10 A 16 AWG (1.5 mm²)
    up to 15 A 14 AWG (2.5 mm²)
    up to 20 A (a fused bank: 4 ports × 5 A) 12 AWG (4 mm²)
    up to 24 A (a Max bank: 4 ports × 6 A) 10 AWG (6 mm²)

    Bank power wiring

    Use 12 AWG or 10 AWG wire from the power supply to each bank terminal. The terminals are rated 30 A each, which is more than a bank can draw: 20 A on a fused bank, 24 A on a Max bank.

    Keep supply-to-controller runs short. For long runs, go up a wire size so the voltage drop stays small.

    Port protection: fuses and eFuses¶

    Every local pixel port has its own overcurrent protection. What kind depends on the model:

    Model Port protection Per-port power control Per-port current reading
    K16, K32 5 A mini-blade fuse no no
    K8-Pro, K16-Pro, K32-Pro 5 A mini-blade fuse yes, per bank of 41 only from Falcon V5 receivers
    K8-Max, K16-Max, K32-Max eFuse, about 6 A yes, per port yes, every port
    K8-PB, K4-PB eFuse, about 5.2 A yes, per port yes, every port
    K8-B, K8-B-Scroller 5 A mini-blade fuse no no
    K8-Pi (v2.x) eFuse, about 5.2 A yes, per port yes, every port
    K2-Pi-Servo, K2-Pi0 eFuse, about 6 A yes, per port yes, every port

    Mini-blade fuses (K16, K32, the Pro boards, K8-B and K8-B-Scroller)¶

    • Each port has an automotive mini-blade fuse (ATM/APM style). All of them are 5 A, on every board. Spares are sold as a 10-pack and at any auto-parts store.
    • Each port has a green LED. It lights when that port has power, which means the bank is powered (and switched on, on Pro boards) and the fuse is intact. A dark LED on a powered bank usually means a blown fuse.
    • Never fit a fuse larger than 5 A. Find the cause of a blown fuse (a short in the pigtail, a pinched wire, water in a connector) before you replace it.

    eFuses (Max boards, K8-PB, K4-PB and the Pi boards)¶

    The Max boards, the K8-PB and the K4-PB replace each port's fuse with an eFuse, an electronic fuse that FPP can switch on and off and read back.

    • Trip point: about 6 A per port (5.6–6.4 A) on the Max boards, the K2-Pi-Servo and the K2-Pi0, and about 5.2 A on the K8-PB, K4-PB and K8-Pi. A hard short trips it at once.
    • When it trips the port turns off and stays off (it latches). The port's LED goes dark, FPP shows a warning, and the Port Status page marks the port as tripped.
    • To reset: fix the cause, then click the port's red icon on Status/Control → Port Status. Power-cycling the controller also resets it.
    • FPP can retry tripped ports automatically. See eFuse auto-retry.
    • FPP reads every port's current continuously. See Current monitoring.

    Power injection on Max boards

    If you inject power at the far end of a string, use the same power supply that feeds that port's bank. A different supply at even a slightly higher voltage pushes current backwards into the port. The eFuse treats that as a fault and switches the port off.

    Bank switching (Pro boards)¶

    On the Pro boards each bank has an electronic power switch that FPP controls. All four ports of a bank switch together. The K8-Pro has had bank switching since v4.0. The K16-Pro and K32-Pro have it from v4.1; on v4.0 boards every bank is on whenever it has power. The revision is printed next to the model name on the board.

    • With FPP's Automatically turn on/off outputs setting on (the default, under Settings → Output), FPP turns bank power off when it has no data to send and back on when a sequence or test starts. That cuts the idle draw of the pixels.
    • The port LEDs show the switch state: a bank that is switched off has all four LEDs dark.
    • The voltage readings in FPP are taken before the switch, so a switched-off bank still shows its input voltage.

    The Max boards do the same thing for each port separately, using the eFuses.

    Ports power up on

    On every board, the ports and banks come up switched on as soon as power is applied, before FPP has started. FPP then takes over and switches off ports and banks that are unused or idle. Treat every port as live whenever its bank's supply is on.

    1. K8-Pro: all revisions. K16-Pro and K32-Pro: v4.1 and later. ↩

    Pixel outputs¶

    The local pixel ports are labelled P1, P2, … on the board (PT1, PT2, … on the K8-PB and K4-PB). Each is a 3-pin, 3.5 mm pluggable (Phoenix-style) connector, and the plugs come with the board. The plug pulls straight off the board, so you can wire your pigtails at the bench and plug them in on site. Spare plugs, in screw or spring-clamp ("Advanced") styles, are sold as pixel connectors.

    Pin Silk Signal
    1 G Ground (−)
    2 D Data
    3 V Pixel power (+), fused, from that port's bank

    The letters G D V are printed next to every port. Check them against your pigtail colours before you power up. Pixel colour codes vary by manufacturer, and a reversed string is usually destroyed at once.

    Wiring a pigtail¶

    1. Strip about 6 mm (¼ in) of each wire. Tin the strands or fit a ferrule.
    2. Loosen the three screws on the plug and insert the wires: ground in G, data in D, and +V in V.
    3. Tighten each screw firmly and tug-test each wire.
    4. With the power off, push the plug fully onto its port.

    Wire size

    The plugs take wire up to 16 AWG (1.5 mm²). Use 16 or 18 AWG pigtails for strings that draw several amps.

    Data signal¶

    • The data output is 5 V logic on every board, whatever the pixel voltage. It works directly with WS2811/WS2812-family pixels at 5, 12 and 24 V.
    • Each data line has a series resistor at the controller. For long first runs (more than about 5 m / 15 ft to the first pixel), use a null pixel or a differential receiver instead of a long bare data lead.

    How many pixels per port?¶

    FPP drives up to 800 pixels per port at 40 frames per second on these boards, on the local ports and on each differential string. Plan power injection for anything over a few hundred pixels. A long string needs power at both ends or along its length, not just from the controller.

    Smart receivers use some of the 800

    When a differential port runs smart receivers, the smart-receiver protocol uses part of that port's pixel time. v2 and V4 cost only a few pixels, but V5 leaves about 725, and v1 loses more with each receiver. See Pixel budget with smart receivers. Receivers in dumb mode cost nothing.

    Port numbering¶

    FPP's port numbers match the silk: P1 is FPP port 1, and so on. The differential ports continue the numbering after the local ports. On a K16, for example, the RJ45 marked 17-20 carries FPP ports 17, 18, 19 and 20.

    Each board page has a map showing where each port is on the board.

    Port LEDs¶

    Each local port has a small green LED next to it:

    LED Meaning
    On The port has power. The bank is powered, and the fuse is intact (eFuse not tripped).
    Off, rest of bank on Blown fuse (K16/K32/Pro), tripped or disabled eFuse (Max)
    Off, whole bank off No power on that bank's terminal, or the bank is switched off (Pro/Max)

    The LED shows power, not data. To check data, run a test pattern.

    Differential ports¶

    The RJ45 jacks labelled with port ranges (9-12, 17-20, 21-24, …) are differential pixel ports. Each carries four pixel strings as balanced (RS-422-style) data over a standard network cable. At the far end, a differential receiver turns the data back into four ordinary pixel outputs and powers them from its own supply.

    Differential data travels hundreds of feet. Use it to put pixel outputs where the props are, instead of running long pixel leads from the controller.

    These are not Ethernet ports

    Do not plug the differential ports into a network switch, router or PC. The controller's one real Ethernet port is the metal-shielded jack with built-in LEDs. The differential jacks are all black plastic.

    Pinout¶

    All differential ports on all boards use the same pinout. The four strings are called A–D in the order of the label. On a jack labelled 17-20, A is string 17 and D is string 20.

    RJ45 pin Signal Cable pair (T568B colours)
    1 String A + orange-white
    2 String A − orange
    3 String B + green-white
    6 String B − green
    5 String C + blue-white
    4 String C − blue
    7 String D + brown-white
    8 String D − brown

    There is no ground and no power on the cable; all eight wires are data.

    Receivers¶

    Any receiver that uses the standard four-strings-per-RJ45 differential pinout above will work. That includes the Kulp Lights receivers and Falcon-compatible receivers.

    • Smart Receiver SRx1: 4 strings.
    • Long-range SRx2 and SRx4.

    Smart receivers and chaining¶

    Smart receivers can be chained: up to six receivers on one differential port, sharing that port's pixel allocation. Give each receiver in the chain its own position on its selector dial, as described in the receiver's instructions, and turn termination on at the last receiver only. Set the chain up in FPP on the port's row in the Pixel Strings table. See the FPP manual.

    Pixel budget with smart receivers¶

    FPP drives up to 800 pixels per port at 40 fps. The smart-receiver protocol shares that time with the pixels, so it lowers the budget. How much depends on which protocol the receivers use (set per port in FPP):

    Protocol Overhead Does 800 px at 40 fps fit?
    None (dumb receivers) none Yes
    v1 about 12 / 36 / 66 px for 1 / 2 / 3 receivers, every frame With 1 receiver only. 3 receivers leave about 757 px.
    v2 none for the first receiver, then about 3–4 px for each one after Yes, up to the 6-receiver maximum (about 17 px for 6)
    V4 a config packet of about 25 px on 1 frame in 12 Just: about 798 px is the safe limit
    V5 a status query of up to about 96 px on most frames No. Keep to about 725 px, or run 800 px at about 36–37 fps

    The budget is per port. All the receivers in a chain share it: output 1 of receivers 1, 2 and 3 together get what's left on that port. An empty receiver position in the middle of a v1/v2 chain also costs one pixel.

    V5 costs the most because it is the protocol that reports back (see below): on most frames the controller sends a query and then waits for the receiver to answer. If you don't need the readings, V4 costs far less.

    Two-way ports (Pro and Max boards)¶

    On the K8-Pro, K8-Max, K16-Pro, K16-Max, K32-Pro and K32-Max the differential ports are bi-directional. Pair 1/2 also carries data back from the receiver. Falcon V5 smart receivers use it to report their eFuse status, current and other readings, and FPP shows them on the Port Status page next to the local ports. Only Falcon V5 receivers report back. Every other receiver works as a normal, send-only receiver on these ports.

    On the K16, K32, K8-PB and K40D-PB the differential ports are send-only. Every receiver works, but none can report back.

    K8-PB and K40D-PB: no V5

    FPP drives the K8-PB and K40D-PB with its BBB48String output, which has no V5 protocol. Set smart receivers on these boards to v1, v2 or V4. A V5 setting falls back to V4.

    Cabling tips¶

    • Use solid-core Cat5e or Cat6 with factory-made or carefully crimped ends. A bad crimp on one pair kills one string and is hard to find.
    • Outdoors, use outdoor-rated (UV/direct-burial) cable and keep the RJ45 ends dry.
    • If a receiver has a terminator setting, turn termination on at the last receiver in a chain only.
    • Each receiver has a test button and data LEDs. Use them to tell a cable problem from a configuration problem. See the receiver's documentation.

    These are the RJ45 jacks on the K8, K16 and K32 boards. The K8-PB, K4-PB and K40D-PB have a 3-pin header or terminal instead, and the K8-B and K8-B-Scroller a 2×3 header; see their board pages (K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB, K8-B, K8-B-Scroller).     RJ45 pin Signal DMX XLR pin

    The jack is wired for standard DMX only. To drive Light-O-Rama, Renard or Pixelnet devices, use an adapter cable that takes data + / − (pins 1 and 2) and ground (pins 7 and 8) to the pins that device expects. Don't connect them with a plain network cable.

    Controls & display¶

    Every board has a small OLED display and five buttons: Up, Down, Enter, Back and Test. The K8-PB and K40D-PB have four: Down and Test share one button, marked Down Test (Down/Test on the K40D-PB). On the status page it works as Test; in menus it works as Down. The K4-PB, PocketScroller v2 and OctoPlus have two buttons, Down (which is also Test, as on the K8-PB) and Enter. The K2-Pi0 has no display or buttons. The BeagleBone Black boards also have a Power button (and the K64D-B and K128D-B a Boot button) for the BeagleBone; see BeagleBone Black. From them you can check status, read the IP address, change modes and run test patterns without a computer.

    The OLED¶

    Every board comes with the OLED fitted. It is a 128×64 I²C display that plugs into a 4-pin socket marked GND 3.3V SCL SDA. FPP sets it up automatically. If the display is ever blank, check Status Display under Settings → System in FPP. See the FPP manual.

    • After a while without a button press, the display turns itself off. Any button wakes it.
    • If you mount the controller upside down, choose a flipped display type in the same setting.

    Status pages¶

    The display normally shows the status page: hostname, IP address(es), FPP mode and what is playing. When the board has sensors, press Enter to step through:

    1. Status: network addresses, mode, playlist and sequence
    2. Sensors: input voltages (VIN1, VIN2, …), temperature, and on Max boards the current per port. Use Up/Down to scroll.
    3. Pressing Enter again opens the Main Menu.

    Test button¶

    Pressing Test on the status page starts a test pattern on every output and steps through the patterns:

    Off → R-G-B Cycle → R-G-B-W-N Cycle → R-G-B Chase → R-G-B-W-N Chase → Off

    While a test is running, Up slows it down and Down speeds it up. Press Test until the display shows Off to stop.

    The test drives every configured port, including the differential ports. It's the quickest way to check wiring before you build a sequence. On boards with switched ports (Pro/Max), FPP powers the ports for the test.

    Note

    The test uses the pixel counts set in FPP's Pixel Strings page. A fresh board is set to 50 pixels per local port (100 on the BeagleBone Black and Raspberry Pi boards), so on a longer string only the first ones light up until you set the real count.

    Line output¶

    The built-in audio uses a PCM5102A 24-bit DAC on the board, so it has no USB jitter or ground noise.

    • 3.5 mm stereo jack (tip = left, ring = right, sleeve = ground).
    • Line level (about 2 V RMS at full volume). It's meant for an amplifier, powered speakers or an FM transmitter's line input. It is not a headphone amplifier, though headphones will work at low volume.
    • FPP lists it as the CapeAudio sound card and selects it automatically. Volume is set in FPP's status page or with the volume commands. See the FPP manual.

    Network, USB & clock¶

    Ethernet¶

    Every PocketBeagle 2 board except the K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB and PocketScroller v2 has one Gigabit Ethernet jack, the RJ45 with built-in LEDs. It connects to the PocketBeagle 2 over USB 2.0, so real throughput is roughly 300 Mb/s. That is far more than a show needs, even with hundreds of E1.31 universes.

    • Plug it into your show network switch or router.
    • Out of the box FPP uses DHCP. The IP address appears on the OLED.
    • K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB and PocketScroller v2: there's no Ethernet jack. Plug a USB Ethernet adapter (or a USB Wi-Fi adapter) into its USB-A port.
    • BeagleBone Black boards (K8-B, K8-B-Scroller, K64D-B, K128D-B, OctoPlus) use the BeagleBone's own 10/100 Ethernet jack, which is plenty for a show. A BeagleBone Black Wireless uses Wi-Fi instead. See BeagleBone Black.
    • Raspberry Pi boards use the Pi's own Ethernet and Wi-Fi. The Pi Zero 2 W under a K2-Pi0 has Wi-Fi only. See Raspberry Pi.
    • For networking topics (static IPs, show networks, reaching receivers or controllers behind FPP), see the FPP manual: Network · Networking overview · Proxies.

    Only one RJ45 is Ethernet

    The DMX and differential RJ45 jacks look the same but are not network ports. Never plug them into a switch or PC.

    The Ethernet jack has two built-in LEDs. Left and right are as seen in the photo at the top of each board page, with the board laid flat and the Ethernet jack on the top edge:

    LED Meaning
    Left Link. On when the cable is connected to a working switch or router port.
    Right Activity. Flashes as network traffic passes.

    If the link LED stays off, check the cable and the switch port before anything else.

    Wi-Fi¶

    The PocketBeagle 2 and the BeagleBone Black have no built-in Wi-Fi (the BeagleBone Black Wireless does). Plug an FPP-supported USB Wi-Fi adapter into a USB-A port to get:

    • Wi-Fi as a second network
    • FPP's hotspot (tethering) mode, for setup in the field

    See the FPP manual's hardware list for supported adapters.

    USB tethering¶

    You can also set the controller up over a USB cable, with no network at all. Connect the PocketBeagle 2's USB-C port to a computer, wait for FPP to start, then browse to:

    • 192.168.7.2 on Windows
    • 192.168.6.2 on macOS or Linux

    Over USB-C alone, only the PocketBeagle 2 itself is powered: the cape's Ethernet, USB ports and outputs stay off until the cape has its own power. That's fine for configuring FPP. On the K8, K16 and K32 boards, the K8-PB, the K4-PB, and all the PocketScrollers it's also safe to keep the USB cable connected while the cape has its own power.

    Not on the K40D-PB, or with a PocketBeagle 1

    Never connect the PocketBeagle's USB port to a computer on a K40D-PB, or on a K4-PB or PocketScroller v2 fitted with an original PocketBeagle 1. It can permanently damage the board. Use a USB network adapter instead. A K4-PB or PocketScroller v2 with a PocketBeagle 2 is fine.

    BeagleBone Black and Raspberry Pi boards: not while the cape is powered

    On the BeagleBone Black and Raspberry Pi boards, never connect the BeagleBone's or Pi's USB port to a computer while the cape is powered. See BeagleBone Black and Raspberry Pi.

    Real-time clock (RTC)¶

    Every board except the K4-PB, PocketScroller v2, K2-Pi-Servo and K2-Pi0 has a battery-backed real-time clock (NXP PCF85263, PCF85363 or PCF8523, depending on the board), so schedules start at the right time even with no internet for NTP. The K4-PB, PocketScroller v2, K2-Pi-Servo and K2-Pi0 have no clock, so set up NTP on them.

    • Every board takes a CR1220 3 V coin cell. On the K8 boards the holder is on the underside of the board. On the K64D-B, K128D-B and OctoPlus it is under the OLED: unplug the OLED to reach it.
    • FPP sets the RTC up automatically from the cape. The RTC setting is hidden because there is nothing to choose.
    • Set the time zone and NTP in FPP under Settings → Time.

    Temperature¶

    A temperature sensor on the board (a TMP1075 on most PocketBeagle 2 boards) reports the board temperature. Some boards, such as the K8-B, K64D-B and K128D-B, have a second one (Temp2). FPP shows it with the other sensors. Boards with a fan header use it to run the fan (see Expansion header & fan).

    Expansion header & fan¶

    Expansion header (Exp)¶

    The Pro and Max boards, the PocketScroller v3 and the PocketScroller-16 have a 2×6, 2.54 mm pin header marked Exp. It brings out the cape's I²C bus and eight general-purpose I/O pins, which FPP names Exp.1 to Exp.8.

    Use them for:

    • Buttons and switches. In FPP, set up GPIO Inputs to start playlists, run commands or trigger effects. See the FPP manual.
    • Outputs to relays (through a driver or relay module), such as a "show running" light.
    • I²C accessories such as extra sensors.

    The pin positions differ between boards, so check the numbers printed next to the header.

    The K8-PB has a different, 24-pin expansion port that drives 16 extra pixel strings through a Falcon F16v4-style expansion board. See its board page.

    Electrical limits

    The Exp pins come from an I²C I/O expander. They are 3.3 V only, and they pull up weakly and can only sink current. Use them with switches to ground, or to drive a transistor or an opto-isolated relay module. Do not connect 5 V or 12 V signals, and don't drive LEDs or relays directly.

    The K16 and K32 have no expansion header.

    Fan¶

    The Pro and Max boards have a 4-pin 5 V PWM fan header marked Fan 5V. It's the standard PC-fan layout:

    Pin Signal
    1 GND
    2 +5 V
    3 Tach (speed sense)
    4 PWM (speed control)

    Use a 5 V 4-pin PWM fan. 12 V PC fans won't spin properly. FPP controls the fan from the board temperature sensor:

    Board temperature Fan speed
    below 40 °C off
    40 °C about 30 %
    50 °C about 60 %
    60 °C and above 100 %

    A fan is optional. Most installations don't need one, but it helps in a closed enclosure in direct sun, or with Max boards running many ports near their limit.

    Boards from revision v4.1 also read the fan's tach signal, so FPP can monitor the fan's speed. Earlier boards control the fan the same way but can't tell whether it is spinning.

    Power terminal¶

    PocketScroller v2¶

    Six HUB75 outputs for LED panel matrices, on a small board that works with either PocketBeagle.

    PocketScroller v2 (v2.2) shown with an original PocketBeagle (PocketBeagle 1) fitted.

    This page covers the PocketScroller v2, printed on the board as PocketScroller+ v2.x (the current revision is v2.3). Like the K4-PB and K40D-PB, it works with both the PocketBeagle 2 and the original PocketBeagle 1.

    How the PocketScroller v2 differs from the other boards

    • No Ethernet on the board. Plug a USB Ethernet adapter or an FPP-supported USB Wi-Fi adapter into its USB-A port. You need one of them to set it up.
    • The USB-A port covers the PocketBeagle 2's SD card slot. To change the SD card you have to unplug the PocketBeagle 2. Either use a PocketBeagle 2 Industrial, which runs FPP from its built-in eMMC (recommended), or fit the PB2 microSD extender, which clears the USB port.
    • The OLED shares pins with output 6. If you use all six outputs, the OLED shows information while the board boots and then turns off once FPP starts. Leave output 6 free if you can.
    • No real-time clock. The board gets the time from the network (NTP).
    • 5 V only, like the other PocketScrollers.

    PocketBeagle 1: never connect its USB port to a computer

    With an original PocketBeagle 1 fitted, don't power or set up the PocketScroller v2 through the PocketBeagle's own USB port (USB tethering). It can permanently damage the board. With a PocketBeagle 2, USB tethering is safe.

    • These are measured, worst-case figures, with random pixel data on every panel. Flat colours and large solid areas refresh faster, by up to about 40 %.
    • The PocketBeagle 1 is faster on longer chains. Past 2 panels per output the PocketScroller v2 runs about 25 % slower on a PocketBeagle 2. If you need the most panels at a high refresh rate, the PocketScroller v3 or PocketScroller-16 is a better fit on a PocketBeagle 2.
    • Number of outputs: on a PocketBeagle 2, one output refreshes at the same rate as all six, so spread the panels evenly. On a PocketBeagle 1, a single output is 25–35 % faster than six, and from three outputs up the rate doesn't change.

    Power terminal¶

    The K8-B has one power terminal, on the bottom edge to the right of the fuses, marked V+ (left screw) and GND (right screw). It powers the BeagleBone and all of the logic, and every pixel port. FPP shows its voltage as VIN.

    Capacity & refresh rate¶

    How many panels you can run depends on how long each chain is and on the colour depth. The table gives the full-frame refresh rate in Hz for 64×32 1/16-scan panels with all eight outputs in use, measured on an OctoPlus at FPP's default brightness (10). Bold values are 60 Hz or more. Italic values are under 60 Hz, where flicker starts to show on camera and to some viewers.

    Panels per output Total panels 12-bit 10-bit 8-bit 6-bit
    1 8 111 132 212 257
    2 16 79 95 138 177
    3 24 59.9 73 99 130
    4 32 48 58 74 96
    5 40 40 48 60 78
    6 48 34 41 50 65
    8 64 26 30 38 49
    10 80 20 24 30 39
    12 96 17 21 26 34
    16 128 13 16 19 26
    • Rule of thumb at 60 Hz with 64×32 panels: 2–3 panels per output at 12-bit colour, or 5 per output at 8-bit (40 panels).
    • Smaller panels go further. A 32×16 P10 panel holds a quarter of the pixels of a 64×32, so a chain of P10s refreshes about as fast as a chain of a quarter as many 64×32s.
    • Refresh depends on the longest chain. Spread the panels evenly across the outputs.
    • A single chain on Output 1 runs up to twice as fast, because Output 1 shares its I/O bank with the panel control lines. Adding a second output costs most of that gain; going from 2 to 8 outputs costs only another 10–15 %.
    • These are worst-case figures, measured with random pixel data. Frames with flat or solid areas refresh faster: a solid fill ran 44 % faster at 3 panels per output, 8-bit.
    • 1/8-scan outdoor panels refresh about the same from 4 panels per output up, and faster on shorter chains (158 Hz at 1 panel per output, 12-bit).
    • Lowering the colour depth is the easiest way to win back refresh on long chains. Unlike the PocketScroller v3, a lower brightness setting lowers the refresh rate a little on the OctoPlus.

    FPP setup¶

    When FPP detects the OctoPlus it sets up an LED Panels output (Input/Output Setup → Channel Outputs → LED Panels) with the OctoPlus wiring, starting with one 32×16 1/8-scan panel on Output 1. Change it to match your panels:

    1. Set the panel size and scan (e.g. 32×16 1/8, 64×32 1/16).
    2. Set the layout: how many panels wide and high.
    3. In the grid, assign each panel to an output and a position in the chain, and set its orientation.
    4. Set colour depth and brightness, then save. FPP restarts the output.

    Powering the BeagleBone: the "power BBB" jumper¶

    The jumper marked Jumper to power BBB from board, on the right-hand side, connects the board's 5 V supply to the BeagleBone. It is fitted at the factory; leave it fitted. With it removed, the BeagleBone needs its own supply in its barrel jack. Never fit the jumper and plug a supply into the BeagleBone's barrel jack at the same time.

    Pixel ports¶

    The sixteen pixel ports are in two rows across the lower part of the board:
    Top row PT1 PT3 PT5 PT7 PT9 PT11 PT13 PT15
    Bottom row PT2 PT4 PT6 PT8 PT10 PT12 PT14 PT16

    Power terminal¶

    The K8-Pi has one power terminal, at the bottom right, marked V+ (left screw) and G (right screw). It powers the Pi and all of the logic, and every pixel port. FPP shows its voltage as VIN.

    Power terminal¶

    • The pixels run at the supply voltage, so both strings must match it: 5, 12 or 24 V.
    • With a 5 V supply, turn its output up to about 5.3 V so the Pi gets a full 5 V; see Raspberry Pi → Powering.
    • The Pi is protected against a reversed supply, but the pixel ports aren't. Check the polarity before you power up.
    • Don't plug a supply into the Pi's own USB power socket while the K2-Pi0 is powered.

    Pixel ports¶

    The two pixel ports, PT1 (left) and PT2 (right), are in the middle of the board. Each is G D V (ground, data, +V), as printed beneath it. Each string can have up to 1600 pixels; see Raspberry Pi → Pixel outputs.

    Each port has its own eFuse, which trips at about 6 A and latches off, and FPP reads both ports' current. When an eFuse trips, FPP marks the port with a red icon on Status/Control → Port Status; click it to turn the port back on once the fault is fixed. The green LED beside each port lights when the port has power. See Current monitoring.

    GPIO / I²C header¶

    The 2×5 header at the left-hand end brings out 3.3 V, ground, the I²C bus and five Pi GPIO pins, numbered on the board by their Pi header pin: 7, 8, 10, 11 and 13. Use them in FPP's GPIO Inputs for buttons or switches to ground, or add an I²C display or clock module. They are 3.3 V only.

    Fitting it to a Pi¶

    On a Pi Zero 2 W (or Zero W), the K2-Pi0's four holes line up with the Pi's: fix them together with M2.5 standoffs.

    It also works on the bigger Pis (3 B+, 4, 5), but there its holes don't line up with anything. The board is held only by the GPIO header, so make sure it can't tip or press down onto the Pi, where it could short something out. Support it with something insulating, and keep it clear of the Pi's components.

    FPP setup¶

    When FPP detects the K2-Pi0 it sets itself up automatically. It enables the K2-Pi0 pixel-string layout (FPP's DPIPixels output, with 100 pixels on each port) and the voltage and current sensors. Set the pixel counts and start channels from xLights or the FPP Pixel Strings page. See Configuring outputs.

    For the first setup, connect to FPP's Wi-Fi hotspot and join the Pi to your Wi-Fi network. Because the board has no clock, set up NTP under Settings → Time so schedules run at the right time.

    Dimensions & mounting¶

    • 65 × 30 mm (2.56 × 1.18 in), 4 holes, 2.7 mm, for M2.5 screws, on the Pi Zero's hole pattern.

    Software (FPP)

    First boot¶

    FPP's own manual covers setup in detail. This page lists what's specific to these boards.

    1. Connect Ethernet. First setup normally goes over the network. With no Ethernet, use a USB Wi-Fi adapter and FPP's hotspot (see below), or a USB cable to the PocketBeagle 2's USB-C port (USB tethering). The K8-PB, K4-PB, K40D-PB and PocketScroller v2 have no Ethernet jack: use a USB Ethernet adapter, or a USB Wi-Fi adapter. The BeagleBone Black and Raspberry Pi boards use the BeagleBone's or Pi's own network; don't use USB tethering on them while the cape is powered. The K2-Pi0's Pi Zero 2 W has Wi-Fi only.
    2. Power up. The OLED shows the IP address once the network is up.
    3. Browse to the IP address (or http://fpp.local) and follow FPP's initial configuration.

    Cape detection¶

    Each board carries an ID chip (the cape EEPROM) that tells FPP which board it's on. On first boot FPP:

    • loads the right pin mapping, so the Pixel Strings page shows your board by name
    • sets up the board's sensors (input voltages, temperature, and port currents on Max boards)
    • selects the built-in audio and the RTC, if the board has them
    • adds a disabled DMX output for each DMX port
    • configures the OLED and buttons

    You can see what FPP detected under Help → About → Cape Info. If the board isn't recognised, see Troubleshooting.

    No Ethernet? Use the Wi-Fi hotspot¶

    With a supported USB Wi-Fi adapter plugged in, FPP starts its own Wi-Fi network when it can't find another network:

    • Join the network named FPP (password Christmas) and browse to 192.168.8.1.
    • On the OLED, Main Menu → Tethering forces the hotspot on or off.

    See WiFi tethering.

    Set the time¶

    The board has a battery-backed clock, but set the time zone (and NTP if you have internet) under Settings → Time so schedules run at the right local time.

    Configuring outputs¶

    From xLights (recommended)¶

    1. In xLights' Controllers tab, add the controller as an Ethernet controller with protocol FPP (or let xLights discover it).
    2. Set the Vendor to Kulp Lights and the Model to your board (for example K16-Max).
    3. Map your props to ports in the Visualiser. Local ports come first (P1, P2, …), then the differential ports in the order printed on the board.
    4. Click Upload Outputs. xLights writes the pixel counts, start channels, colour order and brightness to FPP.
    5. Upload sequences with FPP Connect. See the FPP manual.

    In FPP¶

    The same settings are under Input/Output Setup → Channel Outputs → Pixel Strings. FPP lists the board by name, with one row per port, grouped by bank. See Pixel strings.

    Differential ports appear as groups of four. For smart receivers, use the receiver settings on each group's first row to set up a chain of receivers on that port.

    Defaults

    A new board starts with 50 WS2811 pixels at 50 % brightness on each local port, so the Test button works straight away. Upload your real configuration before the show.

    When an eFuse trips¶

    1. FPP turns the port off, shows a warning banner, and marks it with a red icon.
    2. Find the cause: a short in a pigtail or connector, water ingress, a damaged string, or a string drawing more than about 6 A (about 5.2 A on the K8-PB and K4-PB).
    3. Click the red icon to reset the port. Power-cycling the controller also resets every port.

    If the trip happens again straight away, the fault is still there.

    eFuse auto-retry¶

    Under Settings → Output, two settings make FPP reset tripped eFuses by itself:

    • eFuse Retry Count: how many times FPP retries before giving up (default 0 = off, up to 20).
    • eFuse Retry Time Interval: how long to wait between retries (default 100 ms).

    Retrying helps when a port trips only occasionally, such as from the inrush when a large string powers up. Don't use it to hide a real short.

    Checking pixel counts¶

    On boards with current readings, the Count Pixels button on Port Status estimates how many pixels are on each port from the current they draw, and flags ports that don't match the configured count. It's a quick way to find a dead section partway down a string.

    Automatic power control¶

    Ports and banks power up on. Once FPP is running it takes control. With Settings → Output → Automatically turn on/off outputs turned on (the default), FPP powers ports or banks only while there is data to send, and switches off ports with no pixels configured. So on a Max board, a port that isn't set up in Pixel Strings (or by xLights) is live only for the moment between power-up and FPP starting. That's deliberate.

    Notifications¶

    When an eFuse trips, FPP runs the EFUSE_TRIGGERED command preset, if you've created one, with the port name available as %PORT%. Use it to send yourself a notification (for example through an MQTT or URL command). FPPMon can also alert you to trips while you're away.

    Troubleshooting¶

    Start with the symptom closest to yours. For software problems (playlists, schedules, sequences), FPP's troubleshooting guide goes further.

    Nothing happens at power-up¶

    Check Why
    Is the logic power terminal powered? Only one terminal runs the controller: the upper-left one on the K16 and K32, the right-hand one on the K8. The other boards have one terminal. See Power.
    Is the blue LED beside that terminal lit? If not, there's no voltage at the terminal. Check the supply, the wiring and the polarity.
    Is the polarity right? Reversed power is blocked, not damaging, but the board stays off. Swap the wires.
    Is the second blue LED (5 V) lit? If the terminal LED is on but this one is off, contact support.
    PocketScroller (v3 or -16): is the supply 5 V? A higher voltage trips its input fuse. Disconnect it, wait a minute, and reconnect with 5 V.

    Power LEDs on, but the OLED stays blank¶

    • Give it two minutes. The first boot of a new SD card takes longer.
    • Re-seat the SD card, and try another card or a freshly written image. A worn-out SD card is the most common cause.
    • Check the PocketBeagle 2 is seated fully and the right way round (PocketBeagle 2), or that the BeagleBone is fully on its headers (BeagleBone Black).
    • BeagleBone Black boards: an older BeagleBone (before about 2019) boots its own eMMC instead of the SD card. Hold its Boot button while you power up; see Booting from the SD card.
    • Raspberry Pi boards: check the hat is on all 40 header pins, not shifted by a row or column (Raspberry Pi).
    • BeagleBone Black boards: if FPP was shut down, the BeagleBone stays off until you press the cape's Power button or switch the supply off and on.
    • If FPP is reachable on the network but the OLED is dark, check Settings → System → Status Display.

    Can't find the controller on the network¶

    • Read the IP address from the OLED status page. Make sure your computer is on the same network.
    • Check the Ethernet jack's LEDs. No link light means a cable or switch problem.
    • Make sure the cable is in the Ethernet jack (the one with LEDs), not a differential or DMX port.
    • No Ethernet? Plug in a USB Wi-Fi adapter and use the hotspot.
    • FPP's network troubleshooting covers subnets, static IPs and multiple interfaces.

    FPP doesn't recognise the board¶

    If Pixel Strings shows no board, or Cape Info is empty:

    • Update FPP to the latest release. Older FPP versions don't know the newest boards.
    • Re-seat the PocketBeagle 2 or BeagleBone. The board ID is read over the header pins.
    • Make sure the PocketBeagle 2 boards have a PocketBeagle 2, not a PocketBeagle 1, and the BeagleBone boards a compatible BeagleBone.

    No pixels light¶

    Work from the controller outwards:

    1. Port LED. Is the green LED next to the port lit?
      • Off, and the rest of the bank too: the bank has no power, or is switched off (Pro/Max) because nothing is playing. Run a test (step 2) and check again.
      • Off, just this port: blown fuse, or tripped/disabled eFuse (Max). See Port Status.
    2. Test pattern. Press Test on the controller. If the string lights, the hardware is fine and the problem is in the configuration: pixel count, start channel, or the sequence.
    3. Wiring. Check G D V order against the pigtail, and that the data wire goes to the string's input end (look for the arrows on the pixels).
    4. Voltage. Is the bank voltage right for the pixels? 12 V pixels on a 5 V bank stay dark or flicker.
    5. Protocol and count. In Pixel Strings, check the protocol (WS2811 for most pixels) and that the pixel count is more than 0.

    Only part of a string lights, or colours are wrong¶

    • Colours wrong: set the correct colour order (RGB, GRB, …) for that port.
    • Stops partway: usually a failed pixel. The first dark pixel, or the last working one before it, is usually the bad one. On Max boards, Count Pixels on Port Status helps find it.
    • Ends dim or turn orange/pink: voltage drop. Inject power at the far end, from the same supply (see the power injection warning).

    Flicker or random colours¶

    • Check that all supplies share a common ground with the controller.
    • Make sure only one source is sending data. Turn off any E1.31/DDP output in xLights while FPP is playing.
    • Long first run of bare data wire: add a null pixel, or move the string to a differential receiver.
    • Turn the brightness down to check whether it's a power problem.

    A fuse keeps blowing / an eFuse keeps tripping¶

    There is a real fault on that port. Unplug the string and see whether the port stays up. Then check the pigtail, the connectors and the string for shorts or water. On Max boards, look at the current on Port Status just before the trip. A string drawing close to 6 A on its own is simply too long for one port: split it or inject power.

    A differential receiver is dark¶

    • Is the receiver powered? The RJ45 carries no power.
    • Press the receiver's test button. If it lights, the problem is the cable or the configuration.
    • Swap to a known-good Cat5e/Cat6 cable. A bad crimp on one pair kills one string.
    • In Pixel Strings, check the pixel counts for that port's four strings, and the receiver chain settings for smart receivers.
